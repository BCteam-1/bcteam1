ERP Rescue: What to Do First When You Inherit a Failing Business Central Project

The instinct is to act immediately. That instinct usually makes things worse. Here are the 5 steps that actually come first, in order.

A new CFO joins a company mid-implementation and inherits a Business Central project that's clearly struggling — but "clearly struggling" isn't a diagnosis, and the temptation in the first week is to act immediately, on incomplete information, simply because doing something feels better than doing nothing while the situation is unclear. That instinct, understandable as it is, usually makes things worse. The first move in a genuine rescue situation isn't action — it's a structured, honest assessment before anything else changes.

Step 1: Get an Independent Read on Actual Status, Not Reported Status

The project status you've been given — by the outgoing leader, the implementation partner, or an internal team member — reflects someone's interpretation, filtered through whatever incentives and blind spots they're carrying. Before making any decision, get an independent, outside assessment of where the project genuinely stands: what's actually configured and tested, what data has actually been validated, what scope is genuinely complete versus reported complete.

Step 2: Separate Data Problems From Process Problems From Partner Problems

Struggling implementations usually have some combination of three distinct issue types, and treating them as one undifferentiated mess makes the fix harder to plan. Data problems — migration quality, reconciliation gaps — need a data specialist's attention. Process problems — unclear requirements, scope that was never properly defined — need business stakeholder engagement, not more technical work. Partner problems — staffing turnover, unresponsive support, misaligned expectations — need a direct conversation about whether the current partner relationship is salvageable at all. Diagnosing which category each specific issue actually belongs to determines what kind of help you need next.

Step 3: Resist the Urge to Set a New Deadline Immediately

The pressure to commit to a new go-live date right away, to reassure stakeholders that things are back on track, is real — and it's usually premature. Setting a new deadline before you've actually diagnosed the scope of what needs fixing just recreates the same dynamic that produced the current problem: a date chosen for reassurance rather than built from genuine, verified scope.

Step 4: Decide Whether the Current Partner Continues

This is one of the harder, more consequential decisions, and it shouldn't be made emotionally in either direction — not loyalty to a relationship that isn't working, and not reflexive blame that ignores a partner's genuine willingness to course-correct. The independent assessment from Step 1 should inform this directly: is the core technical work sound with execution or communication problems layered on top, or are the fundamentals themselves compromised in a way no amount of improved communication would fix?

Step 5: Communicate a Realistic Picture Internally Before Committing Externally

Your internal team and stakeholders deserve an honest update on actual status before any new external commitment gets made — a customer-facing deadline, a board update, a budget request. Premature external commitments based on hopeful rather than verified information are exactly what created the credibility problem you're now trying to repair.

Why This Order Matters

Each of these steps depends on the one before it. Deciding on a new timeline before understanding actual scope, or deciding on the partner relationship before separating data problems from process problems, means making a consequential decision on incomplete information — the same pattern that likely contributed to the current situation in the first place.

Where to Start

If you've just inherited a struggling implementation, the single highest-value first move is an independent, structured assessment — not a new deadline, not an immediate partner decision, not a reassuring announcement. Everything else should follow from what that assessment actually finds, not from the pressure to appear decisive before you genuinely understand the situation.
