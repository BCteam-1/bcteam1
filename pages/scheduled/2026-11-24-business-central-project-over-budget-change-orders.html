Business Central Project Over Budget? Read the Change Orders Before Approving the Next One

Quick answer

Before you approve another change order, sort every change order so far into four groups: you asked for something new, you changed your mind, the partner missed something in the estimate, or the work was always in scope. The groups tell you whether the overrun is scope creep, a bad estimate or both, and who should pay for each part.

The third change order arrives with a polite cover note and a number. Approving it feels like the only way to keep going. Declining it feels like a fight. A better move is to pause and sort. Overruns have causes, and the cause decides who owes what. Without the sorting, you pay for everything and learn nothing.

Is it scope creep or a bad estimate?

It is scope creep when you asked for something that was not in the original scope. It is a bad estimate when the original scope covered the work but the estimate undercounted the effort. Both cost money. Only the first one is clearly your cost.

Your signed statement of work is the reference. Read what it lists as included, excluded and assumed.

How do you review change orders?

Put every change order in a table with four columns and one row each:

Change orderWhat changedWhy it was raisedWho owns the cause
CO-1Short descriptionNew requirement, changed mind, estimate miss or in scopeYou, partner or shared

Use these four reasons: new requirement (you added something), changed mind (you reversed an earlier decision), estimate miss (the work was implied by the original scope) and in scope (it should never have been a change order).

Be honest with the "changed mind" column. Late decisions are common and expensive, and hiding them hides the pattern.

Who should pay for each category?

New requirements and changed decisions are normally yours. Estimate misses are usually for the partner to explain, and in-scope items should not be billed twice. What your contract says decides the final answer, so have your lawyer read it. This is not legal advice.

A change order is a commercial agreement. You can ask for an explanation, a revised amount or an alternative before you sign.

What questions should you ask before you sign?

Ask the partner five things: what exactly is changing, which part of the original scope it relates to, what happens if you decline, whether it affects the go-live date, and how much of the remaining budget it consumes.

Ask for the effect on the plan in writing, not only the cost.

When is the problem bigger than change orders?

When change orders keep arriving and each one points to a different cause, the problem is usually governance. Nobody owns decisions, requirements shift, or the design foundation was not settled.

At that point, an independent review is worth more than another negotiation. See our post on switching partners for what to request first.

Taylor’s Take

Overruns are rarely one party's fault. They are usually a pile of small decisions nobody wrote down. Sorting the change orders is not about blame. It is about showing the pattern so the next phase avoids it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal for a Business Central project to need change orders?

Yes. Some change is expected. The warning sign is a steady stream of them with no revised plan.

Can I refuse a change order?

You can decline or negotiate. Check the contract and ask what the effect on the plan would be before you decide.

How do I avoid scope creep?

Agree a decision owner, a change process and a written scope. Review the list of open requests in every project meeting.

Should I pause the project during a dispute?

Often a short pause of design changes is wise. Keep low-risk work, such as data cleanup, moving.
