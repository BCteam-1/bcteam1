Is Business Central Worth It for a 20-Person Company?

Quick answer

Business Central is worth it for a 20-person company when your team already spends real hours on workarounds: re-keying data, reconciling spreadsheets and waiting days to close the month. It is not worth it when QuickBooks or a simple system still does the job without those workarounds. Count the workarounds and the finance hours first, then compare that to the cost.

A 20-person distributor runs on QuickBooks, three spreadsheets and one person who knows how they connect. Nobody has complained about the software. They have complained about the Thursday-night re-keying. That is the real test for a system like Business Central. The question is not "does it have enough features?" It is "how much of our week goes to working around the system we have?"

How do you decide if Business Central is worth it?

Count the workarounds, then put a time value on each. A workaround is any step where a person moves data between systems by hand or keeps a side spreadsheet to fill a gap.

List them for one week. Next to each, write who does it, how often and how long it takes. The total is the cost of your current system that never shows up on an invoice.

What are the signs it is worth it?

Four patterns usually point toward Business Central:

  • The month-end close depends on spreadsheets that only one person understands.
  • Inventory, sales and finance live in different places, so numbers disagree.
  • You need approvals, multiple entities or multiple currencies and your tool does not handle them.
  • Growth plans mean transaction volume and user count will rise, and the current process will not scale with them.

If two or more apply, a serious look is justified.

When is Business Central not worth it?

It is not worth it when your current tool handles your transactions without regular workarounds, and your close is calm. A 20-person company with one entity, simple billing and no inventory may be well served by its accounting package for years.

A new ERP also asks for time from your people during the project. If nobody on your team has that time, wait. A rushed implementation costs more than the system it replaces.

What costs should you compare?

Compare three lines: the license and subscription, the implementation, and the staff time your workarounds consume today. Most comparisons stop at the first two.

Ask for a written estimate that separates configuration, data migration, training and support. See our guide to implementation costs in 2026 for the cost layers. Treat any figure you see online as a range, not a quote.

What should a small company do next?

Run a two-week workaround count, then talk to a Business Central partner with that list in hand. A good partner will tell you if the answer is "not yet".

Bring the list, your current software and your top three reporting questions. Those three items are enough for an honest first conversation.

Taylor’s Take

Do not buy an ERP for its feature list. Buy it to remove specific workarounds, and name them before you sign. A system that fixes a problem you cannot describe will not fix it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Business Central too big for a small business?

Not by size alone. Companies of different sizes use it. The fit depends on process complexity, growth plans and how much manual work your current tools create.

How many users do you need for Business Central to make sense?

There is no minimum that applies to everyone. A five-person company with inventory, multiple currencies and approvals may need it. A twenty-person services firm with simple billing may not.

What is the first step in deciding?

Count your workarounds for two weeks and estimate the hours. That number is the baseline for any comparison.

Can we start with finance and add modules later?

Many companies do. Phasing the project reduces risk, but plan your chart of accounts and dimensions for the whole company at the start.
