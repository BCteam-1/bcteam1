Fixed-Price vs. Time-and-Materials for Business Central Implementation: What Actually Changes

The pricing model changes more than the invoice structure — it changes who absorbs risk when scope inevitably shifts.

Two implementation proposals land on a CFO's desk for essentially the same scope of work — one fixed-price, one time-and-materials — and the natural instinct is to treat this as purely a budgeting preference. Fixed price feels safer; a number is a number. That instinct misses what actually changes underneath the pricing model, and it's worth understanding before choosing based on budget comfort alone.

What Each Model Actually Means

Fixed-price means the partner commits to a defined scope for a defined price, absorbing the risk if the work takes longer than estimated — provided the scope hasn't genuinely changed. Time-and-materials means you pay for actual hours worked, and the risk of the project taking longer sits with you, the client, not the partner.

Neither model is inherently better. They allocate risk differently, and the right choice depends on how well-defined your requirements actually are before the project starts.

Where Fixed-Price Genuinely Protects You

Fixed-price works well when requirements are genuinely well understood and stable — a well-scoped, thoroughly documented project where the main risk is execution time, not discovery of new requirements. In this scenario, fixed-price transfers real risk to the partner, and that protection is worth something concrete.

The catch: a partner pricing fixed-price work builds in a margin for uncertainty, and if your requirements are genuinely well-defined, you may be paying for risk protection you don't actually need as much of.

Where Fixed-Price Becomes a Trap

Fixed-price pricing on a poorly scoped project creates a specific, predictable dynamic: any legitimate new requirement discovered during implementation gets treated as a change order — additional scope, additional cost, often at a premium rate — rather than simply part of the original work. This is not necessarily bad faith on the partner's part; it's the structural incentive fixed-price pricing creates. The result is a project that looked affordable on paper and becomes a series of change-order negotiations in practice, often adding up to more than a time-and-materials engagement would have cost with the same actual scope.

Where Time-and-Materials Genuinely Fits Better

For a project where requirements are still being actively discovered — a business with genuinely complex, not-yet-fully-documented processes — time-and-materials avoids the change-order friction entirely, since there's no rigid original scope for new discoveries to violate. The tradeoff is that budget certainty is lower, and it requires more active client oversight to keep the project moving efficiently rather than drifting.

The Question That Actually Determines the Right Choice

Before choosing based on which number feels safer, ask honestly: how confident are we that our documented requirements are actually complete and accurate? If the honest answer is "quite confident, we've done real discovery work," fixed-price is a reasonable, protective choice. If the honest answer is closer to "we think so, but there's real uncertainty," time-and-materials — paired with active project oversight on your side — often produces a better outcome than a fixed-price contract that turns into a change-order negotiation six weeks in.

A Hybrid Worth Considering

Some partners offer a structure where discovery itself is priced as a smaller, separate fixed-price or time-and-materials phase, with the resulting well-documented requirements then used to build a genuine fixed-price proposal for the remaining implementation. This sequencing reduces the uncertainty fixed-price pricing struggles with, without requiring you to accept open-ended time-and-materials risk for the entire project.
