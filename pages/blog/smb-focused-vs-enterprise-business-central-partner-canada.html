Why an SMB-Focused Canadian Business Central Partner Beats a Generalist Enterprise Firm

A partner whose typical client is far larger than you will structurally deprioritize your project. Three questions to find out where you'd actually rank.

A well-known consulting firm with a roster of enterprise clients can absolutely deliver a strong Business Central implementation — for an enterprise client. Whether that same firm delivers equally well for a 35-person distributor is a genuinely different question, and it's one worth asking directly rather than assuming a strong enterprise reputation transfers cleanly downmarket.

This isn't a claim that bigger firms are worse. It's a claim that client-size fit is a real, structural factor in how a project gets staffed and prioritized — and it's worth evaluating explicitly rather than treating firm size or reputation as a simple proxy for quality.

Why Client Size Affects Staffing, Structurally

Consulting firms allocate their most experienced people toward their largest, highest-revenue engagements — this isn't a criticism, it's how any services business reasonably operates. If a firm's typical client is a 500-user manufacturer paying six figures for implementation, a 30-person company signing a smaller engagement is likely to be staffed with more junior team members, receive less senior oversight, and get less flexibility when the project timeline needs adjustment mid-stream.

None of this shows up in a sales pitch. It shows up three months into the project, when the senior consultant who ran the initial discovery calls has moved on to a larger engagement and a junior team member unfamiliar with the earlier context is now handling day-to-day questions.

Three Questions That Reveal Where You'd Actually Rank

"What does your typical client look like, by revenue and employee count?" A direct answer here tells you immediately whether your project would be near the top or bottom of their client size range. A firm whose typical client is meaningfully larger than you should prompt a follow-up about staffing specifically.

"Who, by name, would be running my project day to day — and what's their current workload?" Vague answers about "a dedicated team" are less useful than a specific name and a straight answer about how many other active projects that person is currently juggling.

"What happens to my project's priority if a larger client has an urgent issue during my implementation?" This question tends to get an honest answer precisely because it's uncomfortable — and the answer tells you something real about how the firm allocates attention when priorities conflict.

Why Boutique Doesn't Mean Under-Resourced

There's a reasonable concern that a smaller, SMB-focused firm might lack depth or resources compared to a larger competitor. In practice, an SMB-focused firm's depth is usually concentrated exactly where a smaller business needs it — practical experience with businesses your size, familiarity with the specific complexity profile of a 20–75 employee operation, and a client roster where your project isn't structurally competing for attention against much larger engagements.

The relevant comparison isn't "big firm versus small firm" in the abstract. It's whether the firm's typical client resembles your business closely enough that their standard way of working — staffing model, response time, project cadence — was actually built around businesses like yours.

What This Looks Like in Practice

A company with 40 employees working with a firm whose median client has 400 employees is likely to experience longer response times, less senior attention, and a project structured around processes built for much larger, more complex engagements than theirs actually requires. The same company working with a firm whose median client has 30–60 employees is more likely to get a project structure, communication cadence, and staffing model genuinely built around a business their size.

The Practical Takeaway

Client-size fit is worth asking about directly and specifically, using the three questions above, rather than assuming firm size or market reputation answers the question on its own. A smaller firm focused specifically on businesses your size isn't a compromise — for many SMBs, it's a structurally better match than a generalist enterprise firm treating your project as a smaller, lower-priority version of their usual work.
